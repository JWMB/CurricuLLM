David Sjölander and Jonas Beckeman interviewed by Olle Landin.  
  
## Vektor, Cogmed  
  
**David**: After we had made those children's games, we also made an adult game called [Rock Manager](https://gamefaqs.gamespot.com/pc/561878-rock-manager). We then had a little pause where we didn't really know what to do, and then we were contacted by a researcher named [Torkel Klingberg](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=LMmiCewAAAAJ&hl=sv), who had the idea that you can train the brain and train working memory.  
  
**Jonas**: I think we started on that fairly early — it was a project that ran in parallel with the other games we made, but at low intensity. I'm pretty sure it was around the turn of the millennium, around 2000 when we first started talking. We made some prototype then and it kept going while we were making Rock Manager, maybe even [Djuphavsjakten](https://archive.org/details/djuphavsjakten_202403).  
  
**David**: That could absolutely be. Yes, exactly — we made a prototype he could use for a research project, and we got good results there. It then became a company, [Cogmed](https://www.cogmed.com/), and a product that trained working memory called [Robomemo](https://www.dn.se/sthlm/spelet-robomemo-hjalper-livliga-barn/). And you could say that [Vektor](https://news.ki.se/these-cognitive-exercises-help-children-boost-their-math-skills-study-shows), which is a later product, builds on the same principle but we combine it with math. [Note: also with exercises for non-verbal problem-solving] So there are math sections where you primarily train your mental representation of a number line. It's very much about dragging things onto a number line. And we combine that with working memory training, which was also present in the old Robomemo.  
  
And this was produced through a non-profit foundation. So it became free for basically all schools in Sweden to use.  
  
**Jonas**: It was fairly big also in Mexico, and it was used a lot in Uruguay — not just in Sweden.

**Olle**: Who financed it then? Where did that money come from, exactly?    

**Jonas**: There was a couple who had used a Cogmed product for one of their children and thought they got very good results. But they also wanted to see an improvement in math results. And they were very well off, so they decided to fund the development of software that could help their child and others.    

**Olle**: So it was together with the people who started this foundation, and they basically financed it all the way. So you were hired by that foundation in some way?    

**Jonas**: Yes, exactly, we were involved in the discussions early on. I don't remember when we decided it should take the form of a foundation specifically, but it was maybe around 2015 when it was established. By then we had already started sketching how the product should look.    

**David**: There were already research projects where we had tested math combined with more working-memory training. But an entirely new product was built that was more—perhaps not commercial—but would simply work in schools. In the earlier working-memory trainings we did, it was common to have a psychologist who helped and coached the children while they trained. Vektor is used by whole classes and teachers can log in and see how it's gone and so on. It's considerably more self-motivating, you could say.    

**Olle**: But that sounds really nice to have that kind of backing. I mean, 99.9% of all games that are made have commercial demands on them in some way. How did that affect development?    

**David**: Above all, it made it much easier to get into schools with something that is free. Otherwise you have to find out who will pay— the municipality, the school, the teachers? Torkel had a lecture tour where he talked about his latest book and mentioned that Vektor existed and that you could use it if you wanted. And a very, very large number of people in Sweden and abroad did so. Did you have any figures?

**Jonas**: I think at most we had, maybe around 50,000 users active at the same time or something like that. But I don't really remember the exact numbers.    
It did require quite a bit of effort. As I said, there were lecture tours and we worked a lot, especially initially, with a number of municipalities in close collaboration to see how it could work in the classroom. It didn't spread by itself; there was a lot of work around it. But when those initial municipalities had talked to their neighboring municipalities, it spread very quickly on its own. And it wasn't just avoiding having to figure out who should pay — avoiding the whole process of paying at all, i.e. just creating an account, helped a lot. Now it's much easier; there are lots of platforms similar to App Stores for schools. But back then, if you had to sign a contract, even for small amounts, I think that would have hindered the spread a lot.  
  
**Olle**: And what did the children — many of them played this then — what did they learn, like, if they used this product?  
  
**David**: This was in the reception/pre‑school class, the year before first grade, basically, and above all it was about getting a very strong link to a number line — i.e., where different numbers lie on a number line. But in connection with doing that you also did addition and subtraction and even fractions. But all the answers you give in the app are, you could say, on a number line. [Note: applies to the primary math exercise, but there were many others]  
  
### Research and scientific evaluation  
  
**Jonas**: We initially looked for research to identify different mechanisms that you could train that had evidence of helping later mathematical understanding. But this visuospatial representation of number was the only concrete thing we found. There's a very clear connection between children's understanding of it and later performance and ability in mathematics. So we focused on that early, and we also saw very good results in research studies based on Vektor later on.  
  
**David**: Very, very few educational materials are actually evaluated in empirical studies with a placebo group and a control group. Here we actually ran exactly the same program as with the math exercises but with different reading exercises. So there have been control groups that have spent exactly the same amount of time but trained something completely different. The way research *should* be done, basically. But it's also very expensive to do research in that way, so I understand why educational publishers don't do this. But I think states should really step in with research funding to evaluate educational materials, to know what works.  
  
**Olle**: That has a huge impact. So if you can improve that more and more by showing it through research, you can find the best ways to teach different things.

**Jonas**: There's a bit of a paradigm shift happening now in how things are taught in school. "Science of Learning" — a concept that is becoming more and more discussed internationally and in Sweden. The Swedish National Audit Office (Riksrevisionen) just a few weeks ago issued a review of how the Swedish school system handles research and empirical evidence in education, with fairly sharp criticism of the Swedish National Agency for Education (Skolverket), the Swedish Institute for Educational Research (Skolforskningsinstitutet) and the government — that they do not evaluate teaching materials and pedagogy critically enough. So I think things will happen on that front going forward.  
  
**David**: I can only add that this doesn't mean that the things used in school don't work. It just means that the research hasn't actually been done to prove what works and what works best.  
  
**Olle**: It can be a bit arbitrary what one has considered to be "good." But what about this product, Vektor? How do you view the whole question of screens in teaching — it has swung between "everyone should have them, they're the future and very positive" and now somewhat "maybe we should remove the screens" and so on. What is your stance on that?  
  
### Screens in education  
  
**Jonas**: That's a difficult question, I think. There's a lot that can be done well with screens, but there are also an incredible number of risks. Very concretely, there's the possibility for students to circumvent the restrictions that are meant to be in place. People find ways to get onto YouTube instead of working with the material they should. And I think that even if you manage to restrict access so they can't get around the limits, there's still the potential on the tablet to do other things. There's an inherent problem with one's focus on the particular app you're working with — I might find a way to get out of this app and into something else. So it's difficult, a difficult area. I think there are contexts where it's really a good way to teach things, but you should be very cautious and make sure it actually works before you start using it on a large scale.  
  
**Olle**: Any other opinions, David?  
  
**David**: Broadly speaking I agree. Of course there are various commentators who argue that pupils will eventually enter a reality where they'll have these phones or tablets and all these distractions, so it's better that they get used to it. But maybe you can do that a bit later in life — not when they're in middle school or so.

**Olle**: So what happened to Vektor?  
  
**Jonas**: Well, the money eventually ran out, and the relationship between those of us active in the foundation and those who contributed funding became a bit problematic. So it's basically shut down.  
  
The foundation still exists, but the product is gone. That's a shame, because it seemed to work very well and was much appreciated. So I've been working a bit in my spare time trying to revive it by recreating the same system in an open-source format, which I intend should eventually be available in the same way, for free.

## Open-source school material  
  
**Olle**: Jonas, what are you working on?  
  
**Jonas**: Yes, after the multimedia years we started, as I said, Cogmed, which we ran for many years. Among other things I was in the US for a couple of years when we started the office there. And then there was this foundation, Cognition Matters, with Vektor. But when that ended I felt I had worked with games and essentially with the same people — David — throughout my career. So I wanted to see whether I measured up in the developer job market, and I started working at a consulting firm, now closed, called tretton37. I was at Telia for, I think, six or seven years. Now I'm employed at a company that primarily works with employee surveys.  
  
But throughout this time I've had side projects that aren't really game-related — maybe they have some game elements — but they're more about education and knowledge that I'm passionate about conveying. For example, a replacement and an improved version of Vektor. And recently, now that I have children in school who are subjected to teaching without textbooks and a lot of ad‑hoc material, worksheets and random web links to various sites, I've become increasingly irritated by the lack of textbooks, which I know is a big problem in Sweden in general.  
  
Books are expensive, and there's a proliferation of various links — which aren't properly quality‑reviewed. I've become more and more annoyed by that, and have started a project whose idea is to eventually produce schoolbooks. It's based on a structure generated by an LLM (large language model) that becomes a kind of framework where teachers and subject experts then go in and edit and improve until it becomes material that can be printed as PDFs or sent to print‑on‑demand — school material that is open source and collaborative. See [CurricuLLM](/README.md)

**Olle**: Yeah, yeah, some kind of open school material then.  
  
**Jonas**: Yes, exactly. And of course it's arranged so that it can also be used as material for apps. So in the Vektor app I'm working on, I can use material from these books and have it as homework quizzes, for example.    
If it's about languages you can have vocabulary quizzes or summarise texts. The dream would be to have material that's so good that it can be used in school. And then, to practise for exams for example, you might use an app at home. I don't really imagine these digital products being something you use in school itself.  
  
### Spaced repetition  
And not least the spaced-repetition element — I've also thought a lot about how my children study intensively before a test and get good marks, but then, a month later, they don't remember a thing of what they studied.    
So that's a very important part — even if you don't have time in school — to revisit different parts you've gone through earlier. That could be something you do as homework, so the app knows what you had problems with and brings those parts up again and refreshes the memory so you get long-term retention of what you learn in school.  
  
**Olle**: Yes, exactly — it's almost strange that we don't have that. As early as the 1980s you could have kept track of this using digital systems, but it doesn't exist, or not really even by hand, I guess.

**Jonas**: Yes, but I think it's hard to get that rhythm into school teaching. You have to cover the subjects one after another, sequentially, and also bring in repetition. I think it's pretty hard to puzzle together that schedule as it is.  
  
**Olle**: Yes, absolutely. But you would probably have learned more if you incorporated it in some way.
